1. Introduction

The traditional group Protozoa (Gr. protos = first; zoon = animal) comprises microscopic, unicellular eukaryotes that carry out all life processes within one cell. The name is retained here because it occurs in the prescribed undergraduate syllabus; it is not treated as a single natural phylum in current phylogenetic classification.

Protozoans were first observed by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek in the seventeenth century. Georg August Goldfuss introduced the term Protozoa in 1818. Modern systems distribute the organisms historically called protozoans among several distant eukaryotic lineages.

Taxonomic status / வகைப்பாட்டு நிலை: “Protozoa,” “Sarcodina,” “Mastigophora,” “Sporozoa,” “Coelenterata,” and “Aschelminthes” are retained only where the syllabus or older examination terminology requires them. Current equivalents and limitations are stated alongside them.

2. General characters

  • Habitat: Aquatic (freshwater and marine), damp soil, or parasitic inside other organisms.
  • Body: Unicellular (acellular); size ranges from about 2 µm to over 500 µm.
  • Grade of organisation: Protoplasmic grade — the entire body is a single cell performing all life activities.
  • Symmetry: Varied — spherical, radial, bilateral, or absent.
  • Nucleus: One, two, or many. Some (e.g., Paramecium) are heterokaryotic with a macronucleus and a micronucleus.
  • Locomotory organelles: Pseudopodia, flagella, cilia, or none (in Sporozoa).
  • Nutrition: Holozoic, holophytic, saprozoic, parasitic, or mixotrophic.
  • Digestion: Intracellular, within food vacuoles.
  • Respiration and excretion: By simple diffusion through the general body surface.
  • Osmoregulation: Through contractile vacuoles in freshwater forms.
  • Reproduction: Asexual (binary fission, multiple fission, budding) and sexual (conjugation, syngamy).
  • Encystment: Formation of a resistant cyst during unfavourable conditions.

4. Locomotion in Protozoa

Protozoans have evolved four distinct modes of locomotion, each associated with specialised organelles:

Pseudopodial movement (amoeboid locomotion)

Pseudopodia (Gr. pseudo = false; podos = foot) are temporary cytoplasmic projections of the body. The protozoan extends a pseudopodium in the direction of movement, and cytoplasm flows into this extension — this is called amoeboid movement.

Four types of pseudopodia are recognised:

  • Lobopodia — blunt, finger-like, containing both endoplasm and ectoplasm (Amoeba).
  • Filopodia — slender, thread-like, containing only ectoplasm.
  • Axopodia — long, radiating, with a central axial rod (Heliozoans).
  • Reticulopodia — branching and anastomosing, forming a network (Foraminifera).

Example: Amoeba proteus uses pseudopodia for both locomotion and feeding (phagocytosis).

Flagellar movement

Flagella are long, whip-like appendages extending from the cell body. Movement occurs by rapid undulations of the flagellum, which acts like a propeller. Protozoans may have one or several flagella.

The ultrastructure of a flagellum shows a 9 + 2 arrangement of microtubules — nine peripheral doublets surrounding two central singlets, all enclosed in a plasma membrane.

Examples: Euglena (one flagellum), Trypanosoma (one flagellum with undulating membrane), Trichonympha (many flagella).

Ciliary movement

Cilia are short, numerous, hair-like structures covering the body surface. They beat in coordinated rhythms — the effective stroke is rigid and propulsive, followed by a flexible recovery stroke.

Two rhythms are observed:

  • Synchronous rhythm — cilia in a transverse row beat simultaneously.
  • Metachronal rhythm — cilia in a longitudinal row beat one after another in a wave, producing smooth locomotion.

Example: Paramecium caudatum — the classic ciliate, moves in a characteristic spiral path.

Gliding and other movements

Some protozoans, particularly Sporozoa, lack conventional locomotory organelles. They move by gliding — a slow creeping movement on surfaces, thought to involve the secretion of mucoid substances or surface adhesion molecules. Euglenoid movement (a squirming of the cell body) is another specialised form.

புரோட்டோசோவாக்கள் போலிக்கால்கள் (pseudopodia), கசைகள் (flagella), குறுங்கசைகள் (cilia) அல்லது வழுக்கி நகர்தல் (gliding) மூலம் இயங்குகின்றன. பாராமீசியம் குறுங்கசைகளால் சுழற்சியாக நீந்துகிறது.

5. Nutrition in Protozoa

Protozoans exhibit five major modes of nutrition, reflecting their enormous ecological range:

ModeDescriptionExample
HolozoicIngestion of solid food particles (animal-like feeding)Amoeba, Paramecium
Holophytic (autotrophic)Photosynthesis through chloroplastsEuglena, Volvox
SaprozoicAbsorption of dissolved organic matter through the body surfaceMonocystis
ParasiticFeeding on living tissues or body fluids of the hostPlasmodium, Entamoeba histolytica
MixotrophicCombination of two or more modesEuglena (holophytic + saprozoic)

Steps of holozoic nutrition

  1. Food capture — by pseudopodia, cilia currents, or cytostome.
  2. Ingestion — by circumvallation, circumfluence, invagination, or import (in Amoeba), or through a cytostome (in Paramecium).
  3. Digestion — intracellular, within food vacuoles. An initial acidic phase kills the prey, followed by an alkaline phase in which enzymes (proteases, carbohydrases, lipases) break down food.
  4. Assimilation — digested products diffuse into the surrounding cytoplasm.
  5. Egestion — undigested residue is thrown out through the body surface or through a permanent opening called the cytopyge (in ciliates).
Reserve food Protozoans store food as glycogen (in animal-like forms), paramylum (in Euglena), leucosin, volutin granules, or lipids.
புரோட்டோசோவாக்களில் உணவூட்டம் ஐந்து வகை — விலங்கு போன்ற (holozoic), தாவரம் போன்ற (holophytic), உறிஞ்சுதல் (saprozoic), ஒட்டுண்ணி (parasitic), மற்றும் கலப்பு (mixotrophic).

6. Reproduction in Protozoa

Protozoans reproduce by both asexual and sexual methods, often showing alternation between the two in a single life cycle.

A. Asexual reproduction

  • Binary fission — the cell divides into two equal daughter cells. May be transverse (Paramecium), longitudinal (Euglena), or oblique (dinoflagellates).
  • Multiple fission (schizogony) — the nucleus divides repeatedly, and the cytoplasm then partitions to form many daughter cells at once. Seen in Plasmodium.
  • Budding — a small outgrowth develops into a daughter individual; it occurs in some suctorian ciliates such as Acineta. Vorticella primarily multiplies by unequal transverse binary fission, producing a free-swimming telotroch.
  • Plasmotomy — a multinucleate parent splits into multinucleate daughters without nuclear division.

B. Sexual reproduction

  • Syngamy — complete and permanent fusion of two gametes to form a zygote. May be isogamous, anisogamous, or oogamous.
  • Conjugation — a temporary union of two individuals (conjugants) for mutual exchange of micronuclear material, followed by separation and fission. Characteristic of Paramecium.
  • Autogamy — self-fertilisation within one individual; the micronucleus undergoes meiosis and two haploid pronuclei fuse.
  • Endomixis — an older descriptive term for internal nuclear reorganisation without pairing or exchange between individuals; its detailed nuclear sequence varies among species and textbook treatments.
  • Cytogamy — two individuals pair, but gametic nuclei are not exchanged; pronuclear fusion occurs within each partner.
  • Hemixis — macronuclear reorganisation without sexual union and without participation of the micronucleus.
Exam tip Distinguish clearly between syngamy (permanent, gamete fusion) and conjugation (temporary, nuclear exchange). This is a frequently asked one-mark and short-answer question.

7. Type study — Paramecium caudatum

7.1 Systematic position
  • Syllabus placement (legacy) — Animalia → Protozoa → Ciliata → Hymenostomatida
  • Current broad placement — Eukaryota → SAR → Alveolata → Ciliophora
  • Lower ranks vary among modern databases; “Protozoa” is not treated as one natural phylum.
  • Genus — Paramecium
  • Species — P. caudatum
7.2 Habit and habitat

Paramecium caudatum is a free-living, cosmopolitan ciliate found in freshwater ponds, pools, ditches, streams, lakes, reservoirs, and rivers. It is especially abundant in stagnant water rich in decaying organic matter and bacterial food. It consumes bacteria, microalgae and other minute suspended particles through its oral apparatus.

7.3 External morphology
  • Size: The largest species of the genus, 170–290 µm long; visible to the naked eye as a whitish or grayish speck.
  • Shape: Elongated and slipper-shaped, with a rounded anterior end and a cone-shaped posterior end. Ventral (oral) surface is flat, dorsal (aboral) surface is convex.
  • Pellicle: A flexible cortical complex formed by the plasma membrane, an underlying layer of flattened membrane-bound alveolar sacs, and the supporting epiplasm. This arrangement maintains shape while permitting controlled flexibility.
  • Oral groove: A prominent, oblique, shallow depression on the ventral surface, extending from the middle of the body to the left side of the anterior end. It leads into a deeper vestibule and then the buccal cavity.
  • Cilia: Numerous tiny hair-like structures covering the entire body surface, arranged in longitudinal rows. Longer cilia at the posterior end form the caudal tuft.
7.4 Body organisation

The cytoplasm of Paramecium is differentiated into an outer ectoplasm (cortex) and an inner endoplasm (medulla).

Infraciliary system

Beneath the pellicle lies the infraciliary system — the neuromotor system of the cell. It consists of kinetosomes (basal bodies of cilia), kinetodesmal fibrils (longitudinal fibres), and kinetodesmata. The kinetosomes are organised in longitudinal rows called kineties. This system coordinates ciliary movement.

Trichocysts

Oval or rod-shaped defensive organelles in the ectoplasm, alternating with ciliary basal bodies. Each consists of an elongated shaft and a pointed tip (barb or spike). When stimulated, trichocysts are discharged as long threads — used for defence and possibly for anchorage during feeding.

Nucleus

Paramecium is heterokaryotic:

  • Macronucleus — bean-shaped, polyploid; controls all metabolic ("vegetative") activities.
  • Micronucleus — spherical, diploid; controls reproductive activities and genetic continuity.
7.5 Digestive system (nutrition and feeding)

Paramecium exhibits holozoic nutrition. The feeding apparatus consists of the oral groove → vestibule → buccal cavity → cytostome → cytopharynx.

Feeding: The cilia of the oral groove and vestibule create a water current that sweeps food particles into the buccal cavity. Selected particles pass into the cytopharynx, while rejected particles are expelled. At the base of the cytopharynx, food vacuoles are formed.

Digestion and assimilation: Food vacuoles circulate in the endoplasm through a process called cyclosis. Digestion occurs in two phases — an initial acidic phase that kills the prey, followed by an alkaline phase in which proteases, carbohydrases, and lipases hydrolyse the food. Digested products are absorbed into the endoplasm.

Egestion: Undigested residue is expelled through a fixed opening, the cytopyge (cell anus), located on the ventral surface behind the oral groove.

7.6 Respiration and excretion

Both respiration and excretion occur by simple diffusion through the general body surface. Oxygen dissolved in the surrounding water diffuses in, and carbon dioxide and nitrogenous wastes (mainly ammonia) diffuse out.

7.7 Osmoregulation

Because Paramecium is a freshwater organism, water continually enters by osmosis. This excess water is expelled by two contractile vacuoles, one anterior and one posterior.

Each vacuole is surrounded by a system of radiating canals that collect water from the endoplasm. The canals empty into the vacuole, which then discharges water to the exterior through a permanent pore. Pulsation rate varies with osmotic conditions, temperature and physiological activity; the central examinable function of both vacuoles is freshwater osmoregulation.

7.8 Nervous coordination and behaviour

Paramecium lacks a true nervous system. Coordination is achieved by the neuromotor (infraciliary) system, which synchronises ciliary beating.

The organism shows both positive and negative taxes to various stimuli — light, temperature, chemicals, touch, gravity. It exhibits an avoidance reaction: on contact with an unfavourable stimulus, it reverses its ciliary beat, swims backward, rotates, and tries a new direction (trial-and-error behaviour).

7.9 Locomotion

Cilia cover the entire body and propel the animal in a characteristic spiral path. Each cilium performs an effective stroke (anterior to posterior) followed by a recovery stroke (posterior to anterior). Cilia of a transverse row beat together (synchronous rhythm), while cilia of successive longitudinal rows beat one after another (metachronal rhythm), producing wave-like motion.

7.10 Reproduction

Asexual reproduction

Transverse binary fission — the micronucleus divides by mitosis, the macronucleus divides amitotically, a constriction appears at the middle of the body, and the animal splits into two daughter individuals within about 30 minutes.

Sexual reproduction

  • Conjugation — two compatible individuals unite temporarily at the oral surface. The macronucleus disintegrates; the micronucleus undergoes meiosis producing four haploid nuclei, three of which degenerate. The remaining nucleus divides into a stationary and a migratory pronucleus. Migratory pronuclei are exchanged between the conjugants and fuse with the stationary pronucleus of the partner. The conjugants then separate (ex-conjugants) and undergo further nuclear reorganisation.
  • Autogamy — self-fertilisation within a single individual.
  • Endomixis, Cytogamy, Hemixis — specialised forms of nuclear reorganisation described in classical ciliate literature.
7.11 Adaptive features and significance
  • Cilia permit rapid, directed swimming in water.
  • Trichocysts provide an efficient defensive mechanism.
  • Heterokaryotic nuclei separate vegetative and reproductive functions.
  • Two contractile vacuoles handle the osmotic challenge of freshwater life.
  • Conjugation provides genetic variation without loss of individuality.
  • Paramecium is a model organism in protozoology, cytology, and genetics.
7.12 Exam-focused summary

Key points to remember:

  • Free-living ciliate, slipper-shaped, 170–290 µm.
  • Pellicle with alveoli; infraciliary system coordinates cilia.
  • Heterokaryotic — macronucleus (vegetative), micronucleus (reproductive).
  • Holozoic nutrition; cyclosis distributes food vacuoles.
  • Two contractile vacuoles for osmoregulation; posterior works faster.
  • Asexual — transverse binary fission; sexual — conjugation.
  • Shows avoidance reaction as behavioural coordination.

8. Type study — Plasmodium vivax

8.1 Systematic position
  • Syllabus placement (legacy) — Animalia → Protozoa → Sporozoa
  • Current broad placement — Eukaryota → SAR → Alveolata → Apicomplexa
  • Class — Aconoidasida
  • Order — Haemosporida
  • Family — Plasmodiidae
  • Genus — Plasmodium
  • Species — P. vivax
8.2 Habit, habitat and hosts

Plasmodium vivax is an obligate intracellular parasite that causes benign tertian malaria in humans. It is a digenetic parasite requiring two hosts:

  • Primary (definitive) host: Female Anopheles mosquito — sexual phase (sporogony) occurs here.
  • Secondary (intermediate) host: Humans — asexual phase (schizogony) occurs here.

In humans, it lives inside liver cells (hepatocytes) and later inside red blood cells (erythrocytes). Mosquito vectors include Anopheles stephensi, A. culicifacies, A. fluviatilis, and A. maculatus. Hotspots are Asia, Latin America, and parts of Africa — P. vivax is responsible for approximately 65% of malaria cases in Asia and South America.

8.3 Structure of the parasite

Ultrastructurally, a young trophozoite of P. vivax shows:

  • A double membrane (pellicle) closely applied to the cytoplasm.
  • Ribonucleoproteins with small dense particles in the cytoplasm.
  • Endoplasmic reticulum as vesicles of variable shapes.
  • Mitochondria with peripheral cristae; number varies with age (one in merozoite, several in trophozoite).
  • Golgi apparatus as small vesicles arranged in rows.
  • A larger nucleus with granular and fibrillar nucleoplasm; centrally located nucleolus.
  • Pinocytic vacuoles functioning as food vacuoles.
  • Hemozoin (malarial pigment) deposited in food vacuoles — a haematin compound produced from the breakdown of haemoglobin.

Characteristics: Anaerobic respiration; both sexual and asexual reproduction; saprozoic nutrition (osmotrophy); lacks locomotory organelles in the mature stage; obligate parasite requiring a host for its life cycle.

8.4 Life cycle — asexual phase in man (schizogony)

The asexual cycle in humans consists of four major phases:

(i) Infection and pre-erythrocytic schizogony

When an infected female Anopheles bites a human, it inoculates sporozoites from its salivary glands into the bloodstream. Within about 30 minutes, sporozoites reach the liver and enter hepatocytes.

(ii) Liver schizogony (exo-erythrocytic phase)

Inside liver cells, the sporozoite rounds up and multiplies by schizogony, forming a cryptozoic schizont that releases thousands of cryptozoites (merozoites). Some merozoites re-invade fresh liver cells (exo-erythrocytic schizogony), while others enter the bloodstream to invade red blood cells. This phase lasts 6–8 days and is clinically silent.

(iii) Erythrocytic schizogony

Inside the red blood cell, the merozoite passes through distinctive morphological stages:

  • Signet-ring stage — young trophozoite with a peripheral nucleus and a central vacuole.
  • Amoeboid stage — actively feeding, irregular form.
  • Schizont stage — nucleus divides repeatedly, forming a rosette of 12–24 merozoites.

The red blood cell ruptures, releasing merozoites along with toxic waste and hemozoin into the plasma. This triggers the classic malarial paroxysm — cold stage, hot stage, sweating stage. In P. vivax, the cycle repeats every 48 hours (benign tertian malaria).

(iv) Gametogony (formation of gametocytes)

After several erythrocytic cycles, some merozoites do not undergo schizogony. Instead, they develop into microgametocytes (male) and macrogametocytes (female) inside red blood cells. These remain in the bloodstream, awaiting transfer to a mosquito. Further development requires the mosquito host.

8.5 Life cycle — sexual phase in mosquito (sporogony)

When a female Anopheles takes a blood meal from an infected person, it ingests gametocytes and other erythrocytic stages. The asexual stages are digested, but the gametocytes survive and continue development in the mosquito's midgut.

  1. Gametogenesis — microgametocytes undergo ex-flagellation producing 6–8 motile microgametes; macrogametocytes mature into non-motile macrogametes.
  2. Fertilisation (syngamy) — a microgamete fuses with a macrogamete forming a diploid zygote.
  3. Ookinete — the zygote elongates and becomes motile.
  4. Encystment — the ookinete penetrates the midgut wall, rounds up beneath the basement membrane, and forms an oocyst.
  5. Sporogony — within the oocyst, repeated nuclear divisions produce thousands of sporozoites. The oocyst ruptures, releasing sporozoites into the mosquito's haemocoel; they migrate to the salivary glands, ready to infect a new human host.

The sporogony cycle in the mosquito takes 10–12 days at 25–30 °C.

8.6 Pathogenesis and prophylaxis

Disease: Benign tertian malaria.

Incubation period: 10–17 days.

Symptoms: Periodic fever with cold-hot-sweating stages every 48 hours; anaemia; splenomegaly; hepatomegaly; relapses due to hypnozoites persisting in the liver.

Diagnosis: Examination of Giemsa-stained blood smear showing Schüffner's dots in infected RBCs; rapid diagnostic tests; PCR.

Treatment: Chloroquine for the erythrocytic phase; primaquine for the liver (hypnozoite) phase to prevent relapse.

Prevention: Vector control — elimination of mosquito breeding sites, use of larvicides and insecticides, insecticide-treated bed nets, mosquito repellents, and protective clothing.

8.7 Exam-focused summary
  • Digenetic parasite — Anopheles mosquito (definitive) and human (intermediate).
  • Schizogony in man (liver + RBC); sporogony in mosquito.
  • Infective stage in man — sporozoite; infective stage in mosquito — gametocyte.
  • Causes benign tertian malaria — 48-hour fever cycle.
  • Hemozoin = malarial pigment; hypnozoites = dormant liver forms causing relapse.
  • Discovered by Charles Laveran (1880); mosquito transmission by Ronald Ross (1897).

9. Type study — Entamoeba histolytica

9.1 Systematic position
  • Syllabus placement (legacy) — Animalia → Protozoa → Sarcodina (Rhizopoda) → Amoebida
  • Current broad placement — Eukaryota → Amoebozoa → Archamoebae → Entamoebida
  • “Sarcodina” grouped unrelated amoeboid organisms by locomotion and is not a current natural class.
  • Genus — Entamoeba
  • Species — E. histolytica
9.2 Habit and habitat

Entamoeba histolytica is a monogenetic (single-host) endoparasite of the human large intestine (colon), particularly the caecum, sigmoid colon and rectum. It causes amoebic dysentery (amoebiasis). The parasite was discovered by Fedor Lösch (1875) in the stool of a Russian patient.

9.3 Structure — three morphological forms

(a) Trophozoite (magna form)

  • Size 20–40 µm; irregular and constantly changing shape.
  • Clear ectoplasm with blunt, finger-like pseudopodia; granular endoplasm.
  • Single spherical nucleus with a central karyosome and peripheral chromatin granules.
  • Endoplasm contains ingested red blood cells — a diagnostic feature.
  • Pathogenic, tissue-invading stage.

(b) Precystic form (minuta form)

  • Size 10–20 µm; smaller and less active.
  • Does not contain ingested RBCs.
  • Transitional stage, found in the intestinal lumen.

(c) Cyst

  • Size 10–15 µm; spherical, with a smooth resistant wall.
  • Mature cyst is quadrinucleate — contains four nuclei.
  • Young cyst contains chromatoid bars (rod-shaped RNA bodies) and a glycogen vacuole.
  • Infective stage; passed out in the faeces of the host.
9.4 Life cycle

Entamoeba histolytica has a simple, monogenetic (single-host) life cycle.

  1. Infection — humans are infected by ingesting mature quadrinucleate cysts through contaminated food or water (faecal-oral route).
  2. Excystation — in the small intestine, the cyst wall dissolves. The liberated metacyst undergoes nuclear and cytoplasmic divisions to form eight small metacystic trophozoites.
  3. Colonisation — trophozoites migrate to the large intestine, feed on intestinal contents and multiply by binary fission.
  4. Tissue invasion — trophozoites secrete proteolytic enzymes (cysteine proteinases) that lyse the intestinal mucosa, producing flask-shaped ulcers. They may enter the submucosa and, via portal circulation, reach the liver, causing amoebic liver abscess.
  5. Encystation — some trophozoites round up in the intestinal lumen, secrete a cyst wall, and undergo two successive nuclear divisions to produce the quadrinucleate infective cyst.
  6. Transmission — cysts are passed out in faeces; they can survive for weeks in moist environments and infect new hosts through contaminated food, water, or unwashed hands.
9.5 Pathogenesis and prophylaxis

Disease: Amoebiasis — intestinal and extraintestinal forms.

Intestinal amoebiasis: Abdominal cramps, bloody-mucoid diarrhoea ("amoebic dysentery"), tenesmus, dehydration. Characteristic flask-shaped ulcers in the colon.

Extraintestinal amoebiasis: Liver abscess (most common), lung abscess, brain abscess, cutaneous amoebiasis.

Diagnosis: Microscopic examination of stool for trophozoites (showing ingested RBCs) and cysts; serological tests; stool antigen detection; imaging for liver abscess.

Treatment: Metronidazole (for tissue-invading trophozoites), followed by a luminal agent such as diloxanide furoate or paromomycin (to eliminate cysts from the intestine).

Prevention: Safe drinking water, proper sanitation, handwashing, thorough washing of vegetables and fruits, avoidance of raw food in endemic areas, control of human faecal contamination of water sources.

9.6 Exam-focused summary
  • Monogenetic parasite of the human large intestine.
  • Three forms — trophozoite (pathogenic), precystic, cyst (infective).
  • Mature cyst is quadrinucleate — the hallmark of E. histolytica.
  • Transmission by faecal-oral route through contaminated food/water.
  • Causes amoebic dysentery and amoebic liver abscess.
  • Diagnostic feature of trophozoite — ingested red blood cells in cytoplasm.
  • Clinical treatment is regimen-dependent: invasive disease requires a tissue-active agent, followed by a luminal amoebicide to eradicate intestinal carriage; treatment must be directed by a qualified clinician.

Harmful aspects

  • Plasmodium — malaria (still one of the deadliest parasitic diseases globally).
  • Entamoeba histolytica — amoebiasis, amoebic liver abscess.
  • Trypanosoma — African sleeping sickness, Chagas disease.

Short-answer questions

3 marksDifferentiate between syngamy and conjugation.
Syngamy is the permanent and complete fusion of two gametes forming a diploid zygote (e.g., in Plasmodium). Conjugation is a temporary union of two individuals (conjugants) during which only micronuclear material is exchanged; after exchange, the conjugants separate as genetically renewed individuals (e.g., in Paramecium). Thus syngamy produces a new individual (zygote), whereas conjugation merely rejuvenates existing individuals.
3 marksExplain the structure and function of the contractile vacuole in Paramecium.
Two contractile vacuoles lie one near each end of the body. Each is surrounded by radiating canals that collect excess water from the endoplasm and deliver it to the central vacuole, which then discharges the water through a permanent pore. They are osmoregulatory organelles that compensate for continuous osmotic water entry in the freshwater habitat; their pulsation rate changes with environmental and physiological conditions.

Five-mark question

5 marksDescribe the structure and feeding mechanism of Paramecium caudatum.
Paramecium caudatum is a slipper-shaped, free-living ciliate of freshwater habitats, 170–290 µm long. Its body is covered by an elastic, three-layered pellicle with alveoli and is uniformly ciliated; the posterior cilia form a caudal tuft. Beneath the pellicle lies the infraciliary system of kinetosomes and kinetodesmata, which coordinates ciliary movement. The cytoplasm is differentiated into ectoplasm (with trichocysts) and endoplasm (with the two nuclei, food vacuoles, and contractile vacuoles). It possesses a bean-shaped polyploid macronucleus for vegetative functions and a spherical diploid micronucleus for reproduction. Feeding is holozoic. Cilia of the oral groove drive a current of water bearing bacteria and small protozoans into the vestibule and buccal cavity. At the cytostome, food vacuoles are formed. These vacuoles circulate by cyclosis through the endoplasm; digestion occurs first in an acidic phase that kills the prey, followed by an alkaline enzymatic phase that hydrolyses food. Digested nutrients are absorbed, and undigested residue is egested through the cytopyge.

Ten-mark question

10 marksDescribe the life cycle of Plasmodium vivax with reference to its two hosts.
Plasmodium vivax is a digenetic parasite whose life cycle alternates between a human intermediate host (asexual schizogony) and a female Anopheles mosquito definitive host (sexual sporogony). Infection in man: When an infected female mosquito bites a human, sporozoites from its salivary glands enter the bloodstream and within 30 minutes reach the liver. Pre-erythrocytic (liver) schizogony: Inside hepatocytes the sporozoite multiplies, forming a cryptozoic schizont that releases cryptozoites. Some invade fresh liver cells (exo-erythrocytic schizogony); others enter the bloodstream. Erythrocytic schizogony: Inside red blood cells, the parasite passes through signet-ring, amoeboid, and schizont stages, producing 12–24 merozoites. Rupture of the RBC releases merozoites, haemozoin, and toxins, producing the classic malarial paroxysm every 48 hours — benign tertian malaria. Gametogony: After several cycles, some merozoites develop into micro- and macrogametocytes. In the mosquito: Gametocytes ingested during a blood meal undergo gametogenesis in the midgut — microgametocytes ex-flagellate into microgametes, macrogametocytes mature into macrogametes. Syngamy forms a diploid zygote that elongates into a motile ookinete, penetrates the midgut wall, and forms an oocyst. Within the oocyst, repeated divisions produce thousands of sporozoites (sporogony) which migrate to the salivary glands, ready to infect the next human. The complete cycle requires both hosts and produces the characteristic periodic fever pattern.

1. Phylum Porifera — General Characters

Porifera are pore-bearing aquatic animals commonly called sponges. They are mostly marine, sessile, multicellular animals with cellular grade of organisation. Although they are animals, they lack true tissues, organs, mouth, digestive cavity and nervous system. Their body is organised around a water-current system: water enters through numerous ostia, passes through canals and chambers, and leaves through one or more oscula.

போரிபெரா என்பது துளைகள் கொண்ட நீர்வாழ் முதுகெலும்பில்லா விலங்குகள். உண்மையான திசுக்கள் மற்றும் உறுப்புகள் இல்லை. ஆஸ்டியா வழியாக நீர் உடலுக்குள் நுழைந்து, ஆஸ்குலம் வழியாக வெளியேறும்.
  • Habitat: mostly marine; a few freshwater forms such as Spongilla.
  • Habit: sessile; attached to rocks, shells or submerged objects.
  • Symmetry: usually asymmetrical; some are radially symmetrical.
  • Organisation: cellular grade; cells are specialised but true tissues are absent.
  • Body wall: outer pinacoderm, inner choanoderm and gelatinous mesohyl.
  • Canal system: unique water-current system used for feeding, respiration, excretion and reproduction.
  • Nutrition: filter feeding; digestion is intracellular.
  • Respiration and excretion: by diffusion through body cells.
  • Skeleton: calcareous spicules, siliceous spicules or spongin fibres.
  • Reproduction: asexual by budding, fragmentation and gemmules; sexual by gametes. Development includes a free-swimming larva.

5-mark answer: General characters of Porifera
  1. Poriferans are aquatic, mostly marine, sessile animals called sponges.
  2. The body has many ostia and one or more oscula; water-current system is characteristic.
  3. They show cellular grade of organisation and lack true tissues and organs.
  4. The body wall contains pinacocytes, choanocytes and amoebocytes in mesohyl.
  5. Choanocytes create water current and help in food capture.
  6. Skeleton is made of calcareous/siliceous spicules or spongin fibres.
  7. Digestion is intracellular; respiration and excretion occur by diffusion.
  8. Reproduction is both asexual and sexual; larval forms are free-swimming.

2. Type Study — Sycon

Sycon is a marine calcareous sponge commonly found attached to rocks, shells and submerged objects. It is usually vase-shaped or cylindrical, with a terminal osculum surrounded by a fringe of spicules. The body wall is folded to form a syconoid canal system, which is more advanced than the simple asconoid type.

Sycon என்பது கடல் நீரில் வாழும் கல்கேரியஸ் ஸ்பாஞ். இதன் உடலில் சைக்கனாய்டு கால்வாய் அமைப்பு காணப்படும். இது தேர்வில் மிகவும் முக்கியமான வரைபடக் கேள்வி.
Systematic position of Sycon
  • Kingdom — Animalia
  • Phylum — Porifera
  • Class — Calcarea
  • Order — Leucosolenida (older syllabus term: Heterocoela)
  • Genus — Sycon
Habit, habitat and external morphology
  • Habit and habitat: marine, sessile and colonial or solitary; attached to hard substrata.
  • Body shape: cylindrical or vase-shaped with a broad base and terminal osculum.
  • Surface: rough due to projecting spicules.
  • Openings: numerous dermal ostia on surface; one terminal osculum at the free end.
Canal system in Sycon

The water-current pathway is:

Outside water → dermal ostia → incurrent canals → prosopyles → radial canals → apopyles → spongocoel → osculum.

The radial canals are lined with choanocytes. Their flagella beat continuously, drawing water into the body. Food particles are trapped by collars of choanocytes; oxygen diffuses into cells; carbon dioxide and nitrogenous wastes are removed with outgoing water.

Reproduction in Sycon

Asexual reproduction occurs by budding. Sexual reproduction occurs by formation of sperms and ova. Sperms are released into water and enter another sponge through the canal system. Fertilisation is internal. Development includes a free-swimming amphiblastula larva, which later settles and develops into a young sponge.

8-mark answer: Canal system in Sycon

Introduction: The canal system is the most characteristic feature of sponges. In Sycon, the body wall is folded to form a syconoid canal system.

  1. Water enters through numerous dermal ostia present on the external surface.
  2. It passes into incurrent canals, which are not lined by choanocytes.
  3. From incurrent canals, water enters radial canals through small openings called prosopyles.
  4. Radial canals are lined by choanocytes. Their flagella generate water current and trap food particles.
  5. Water passes from radial canals into the spongocoel through apopyles.
  6. Finally, water leaves through the terminal osculum.
  7. The canal system helps in feeding, respiration, excretion, removal of wastes and transport of gametes/larvae.
  8. The folded syconoid arrangement is more efficient than the simple asconoid condition.

3. Phylum Cnidaria — General Characters (Coelenterata: legacy syllabus term)

Coelenterates, now commonly placed under Cnidaria, are aquatic, mostly marine animals with tissue-grade organisation. They are radially symmetrical, diploblastic animals possessing an internal gastrovascular cavity called the coelenteron. Their diagnostic feature is the presence of cnidocytes, specialised stinging cells containing nematocysts.

சீலெண்டரேட்டா / நிடேரியா விலங்குகளில் கதிர் சமச்சீர், இரு கருவடுக்கு உடலமைப்பு, கோஎலென்டெரான் மற்றும் நிடோசைட்டுகள் காணப்படும். நிடோசைட்டுகள் பாதுகாப்புக்கும் உணவுப் பிடிப்புக்கும் பயன்படுகின்றன.
  • Mostly marine; a few freshwater forms such as Hydra.
  • Radial symmetry is common.
  • Diploblastic body wall: ectoderm and endoderm separated by mesoglea.
  • Coelenteron acts in digestion and distribution of food.
  • Mouth is surrounded by tentacles.
  • Cnidocytes with nematocysts are used for food capture, defence and attachment.
  • Nervous system is a diffuse nerve net.
  • Digestion is both extracellular and intracellular.
  • Two basic forms are polyp and medusa.
  • Reproduction may be asexual by budding and sexual by gametes.

  1. Cnidarians are aquatic, mostly marine animals with tissue-grade organisation.
  2. They are diploblastic and radially symmetrical.
  3. The body wall has ectoderm, endoderm and non-cellular mesoglea.
  4. A central coelenteron acts as gastrovascular cavity.

4. Type Study — Obelia

Obelia is a marine colonial hydrozoan found attached to seaweeds, rocks and shells. It shows a clear division of labour among zooids and is therefore a standard example for polymorphism. The colony is branching and plant-like, but each branch is a living animal colony connected by a common gastrovascular cavity.

Obelia என்பது கடல் நீரில் வாழும் காலனி அமைப்புடைய நிடேரியன். இதில் உணவு பெறும் ஹைட்ராந்த் மற்றும் இனப்பெருக்கம் செய்யும் கோனாங்கியம் போன்ற வேறுபட்ட zooids காணப்படும்.
Systematic position of Obelia
  • Kingdom — Animalia
  • Phylum — Cnidaria / Coelenterata
  • Class — Hydrozoa
  • Order — Leptothecata / Thecata
  • Genus — Obelia
Structure of Obelia colony
  • Hydrorhiza: basal root-like part attached to substratum.
  • Hydrocaulus: erect branching stem of the colony.
  • Coenosarc: living soft inner part containing gastrovascular cavity.
  • Perisarc: non-living chitinous protective covering.
  • Hydranth / gastrozooid: feeding zooid with mouth and tentacles.
  • Gonangium / gonozooid: reproductive zooid producing medusa buds.
Life cycle and metagenesis

Colony → gonangium → medusa buds → free medusae → gametes → fertilisation → zygote → planula larva → new colony.

The sedentary polyp colony reproduces asexually by budding and produces medusae. Free medusae reproduce sexually by forming gametes. This alternation of asexual polyp generation and sexual medusa generation is called metagenesis.

8-mark answer: Structure and life cycle of Obelia

Introduction: Obelia is a colonial marine hydrozoan. It is important because it shows polymorphism and metagenesis.

  1. The colony is attached to substratum by hydrorhiza.
  2. Vertical branches called hydrocauli arise from the hydrorhiza.
  3. The living inner part is coenosarc; the non-living protective covering is perisarc.
  4. Hydranths are feeding zooids. Each hydranth has mouth and tentacles.
  5. Gonangia are reproductive zooids. Each contains blastostyle bearing medusa buds.
  6. Medusa buds are released as free-swimming medusae.
  7. Medusae produce gametes. Fertilisation forms a zygote.
  8. The zygote develops into a ciliated planula larva, which settles and forms a new colony.

Conclusion: Alternation of asexual polyp colony and sexual medusa is called metagenesis.

5. Polymorphism and Coral Reefs

Polymorphism is the occurrence of structurally and functionally different individuals or zooids within the same species or colony. It is well developed in colonial hydrozoans. In Obelia, hydranths perform nutrition and gonangia perform reproduction. This division of labour increases efficiency of the colony.

5-mark answer: Polymorphism in Coelenterata
  1. Polymorphism means occurrence of different kinds of zooids in the same colony.
  2. Each zooid is specialised for a particular function.
  3. In Obelia, hydranths are feeding zooids.
  4. Gonangia are reproductive zooids producing medusa buds.
  5. Medusa is the free-swimming sexual form.
  6. Polymorphism brings division of labour and increases colonial efficiency.

Coral reefs are massive calcareous structures formed mainly by colonial anthozoan polyps. Reef-building corals secrete calcium carbonate skeletons and usually contain symbiotic photosynthetic algae called zooxanthellae.

  • Fringing reef: grows close to the shore.
  • Barrier reef: separated from the shore by a lagoon.
  • Atoll: ring-shaped reef enclosing a lagoon.
  • Importance: biodiversity hotspot, fishery support, coastal protection and tourism.
  • Threats: bleaching, ocean warming, acidification, pollution, sedimentation and destructive fishing.
8-mark answer: Coral reefs — formation, types, importance and threats

Formation: Coral reefs are formed by colonial coral polyps that secrete calcium carbonate skeletons. Symbiotic zooxanthellae provide nutrition through photosynthesis and help reef growth in shallow, warm, clear marine waters.

  1. Fringing reef: develops close to the coast.
  2. Barrier reef: separated from land by a lagoon.
  3. Atoll: ring-shaped reef around a lagoon, often formed around sinking volcanic islands.
  4. Ecological importance: reefs are biodiversity hotspots and provide habitat, breeding and nursery grounds.
  5. Economic importance: support fisheries, tourism and coastal livelihoods.
  6. Protective role: reduce wave energy and protect shorelines from erosion.
  7. Threats: bleaching, climate warming, ocean acidification, sedimentation, pollution and destructive fishing.
  8. Conservation: marine protected areas, pollution control, sustainable fishing and climate action are needed.

1. Introduction to Helminthes

Helminths are worm-like animals, many of which are parasitic in humans, domestic animals and livestock. In undergraduate zoology, Unit III usually covers two major worm groups: Platyhelminthes or flatworms, and Aschelminthes/Nematoda or roundworms. Their study is important because they show advanced parasitic adaptations, complex life cycles, host specificity, larval migration, and major public-health relevance.

Helminth parasites reduce host fitness by causing anaemia, malnutrition, tissue damage, intestinal obstruction, reduced growth, impaired learning in children, and economic loss in livestock. The major exam focus is not only morphology, but also life-cycle logic: infective stage, diagnostic stage, intermediate host, definitive host, mode of transmission, pathogenicity and prevention.

2. Phylum Platyhelminthes — General Characters

  • Body is dorsoventrally flattened, hence the name flatworms.
  • They are triploblastic, bilaterally symmetrical and show organ-system grade of organisation.
  • They are acoelomate; the space between body wall and gut is filled with parenchyma.
  • Body may be free-living, as in Planaria, or parasitic, as in Fasciola and Taenia.
  • Digestive system is incomplete or absent. In trematodes it has mouth and branched intestine but no anus; in cestodes digestive system is absent.
  • Respiratory and circulatory systems are absent; exchange occurs by diffusion.
  • Excretory system consists of flame cells or protonephridia.
  • Nervous system is ladder-like with anterior ganglia and longitudinal nerve cords.
  • Most forms are hermaphroditic; reproductive system is well developed in parasitic forms.
  • Life cycles may involve one or more hosts and several larval stages.

3. Type Study — Fasciola hepatica / Liver Fluke

3.1 Systematic position
  • Kingdom — Animalia
  • Phylum — Platyhelminthes
  • Class — Trematoda
  • Order — Digenea
  • Genus — Fasciola
  • Species — F. hepatica
3.2 Habit, habitat and host relationship

Fasciola hepatica is an endoparasitic trematode commonly called the sheep liver fluke. Adult worms live in the bile ducts of sheep, goats, cattle and sometimes humans. The definitive host harbours the adult parasite, while a suitable freshwater snail of the family Lymnaeidae acts as the intermediate host; older syllabi commonly use Lymnaea as the representative genus. Infection occurs when the definitive host ingests aquatic vegetation carrying metacercariae, the infective stage.

3.3 External morphology
  • Adult body is leaf-like, dorsoventrally flattened and brownish.
  • Body is broader anteriorly and tapering posteriorly.
  • Two suckers are present: oral sucker around the mouth and ventral sucker or acetabulum for attachment.
  • The body surface is covered by a tough tegument with minute spines that protect the parasite from host enzymes.
  • Mouth opens into pharynx, oesophagus and two highly branched intestinal caeca.
  • Sexual organs are highly developed because reproduction is central to parasitic success.
3.4 Internal organisation

The digestive system is incomplete, with no anus. The branched intestine distributes food through the body. Respiration is mainly anaerobic. The excretory system has flame cells that drain into excretory ducts and an excretory pore. The nervous system is simple, consisting of cerebral ganglia and longitudinal nerve cords. The reproductive system is hermaphroditic, including testes, ovary, vitellaria, uterus and genital pore.

3.5 Life cycle of Fasciola
  1. Adult flukes in bile ducts produce eggs, which pass through bile into intestine and leave the host with faeces.
  2. In water, the egg develops into a ciliated larva called miracidium.
  3. Miracidium penetrates a suitable freshwater lymnaeid snail.
  4. Inside the snail, it passes through sporocyst → redia → cercaria stages.
  5. Cercaria escapes from the snail and encysts on aquatic vegetation as metacercaria.
  6. The definitive host becomes infected by eating vegetation containing metacercariae.
  7. Young flukes excyst in the intestine, migrate to the liver, and mature in bile ducts.
Exam must-write Infective stage for sheep/human: metacercaria. Diagnostic stage: egg in faeces. Intermediate host: a freshwater lymnaeid snail (often written as Lymnaea in traditional syllabi).
3.6 Pathogenicity and control

Fascioliasis causes liver damage, obstruction of bile ducts, anaemia, weakness, weight loss and reduced productivity in livestock. Control measures include avoiding raw aquatic vegetation, proper drainage of grazing areas, snail control, veterinary treatment of infected animals, and hygienic disposal of faeces.

4. Phylum Nematoda — General Characters (Aschelminthes: legacy syllabus term)

  • Body is cylindrical, elongated, unsegmented and tapering at both ends.
  • They are triploblastic, bilaterally symmetrical and pseudocoelomate.
  • Body is covered by a tough, resistant cuticle.
  • Digestive system is complete with mouth and anus.
  • Circulatory and respiratory systems are absent.
  • Excretory system may consist of gland cells or H-shaped canals.
  • Sexes are usually separate; sexual dimorphism is common.
  • Many are free-living in soil and water, while several are important parasites.
  • Growth involves moulting of the cuticle.

5. Type Study — Ascaris lumbricoides

5.1 Systematic position
  • Kingdom — Animalia
  • Phylum — Nematoda (older syllabus grouping: Aschelminthes)
  • Class — Chromadorea (older terms: Secernentea / Phasmidia)
  • Order — Rhabditida; Ascaridomorpha lineage (older rank: Ascaridida)
  • Genus — Ascaris
  • Species — A. lumbricoides
5.2 Habit and habitat

Ascaris lumbricoides is an intestinal endoparasite of humans, especially common where sanitation is poor. Adults live in the small intestine. Eggs leave the host in faeces and become infective in soil. Infection occurs by swallowing embryonated eggs through contaminated food, water or dirty hands.

5.3 External morphology and sexual dimorphism
  • Body is long, cylindrical, unsegmented and tapering at both ends.
  • The body wall has thick cuticle, hypodermis and longitudinal muscles.
  • Mouth is terminal and surrounded by three lips.
  • Female is longer and straighter, with genital aperture on ventral surface near the anterior one-third.
  • Male is smaller and has a curved posterior end with copulatory spicules.
5.4 Internal organisation

The alimentary canal is complete with mouth, muscular pharynx, intestine, rectum and anus/cloaca. The pseudocoelom acts as a hydrostatic skeleton. Respiratory and circulatory systems are absent. Excretion occurs through an H-shaped excretory canal system. The nervous system includes a nerve ring around the pharynx and longitudinal nerve cords. Reproduction is sexual and sexes are separate.

5.5 Life cycle of Ascaris
  1. Adult male and female worms live in the human small intestine.
  2. Fertilised eggs are passed out with faeces.
  3. In warm, moist soil, eggs develop into embryonated infective eggs.
  4. Human infection occurs by swallowing infective eggs with contaminated food or water.
  5. Larvae hatch in intestine, penetrate intestinal wall and enter blood circulation.
  6. They migrate through liver, heart and lungs.
  7. Larvae enter alveoli, move up the trachea, are swallowed again, and return to small intestine.
  8. They mature into adults and repeat the cycle.
Exam must-write Infective stage: embryonated egg. Diagnostic stage: egg in stool. No intermediate host is required.
5.6 Pathogenicity and control

Ascariasis may cause abdominal discomfort, malnutrition, allergic reactions, cough during larval migration, and intestinal obstruction in heavy infections. Prevention includes sanitary disposal of faeces, washing hands, cleaning vegetables, safe drinking water, deworming programmes and health education.

6. Parasitic Adaptations in Helminths

  • Protective covering: Tegument in flukes and cuticle in nematodes protect against host enzymes.
  • Attachment organs: Suckers, hooks, lips or spines help the parasite remain attached to host tissues.
  • High reproductive capacity: Large number of eggs compensate for heavy mortality during transmission.
  • Anaerobic respiration: Suits oxygen-poor internal host environment.
  • Reduction of unnecessary systems: Sensory and locomotory organs are reduced in many endoparasites.
  • Complex life cycles: Intermediate hosts and resistant stages increase transmission opportunities.

1. Phylum Annelida — General Characters

Annelida includes segmented worms with triploblastic, bilaterally symmetrical, organ-system grade organisation. The body shows true metamerism, and most forms possess a true coelom lined by mesoderm. Annelids mark a major evolutionary step because segmentation allows regional specialization, efficient locomotion and better internal organization.

அன்னெலிடா விலங்குகளில் உடல் உண்மையான வளையப் பிரிவுகளாகப் பிரிந்துள்ளது. இவை இருபக்கச் சமச்சீர், மூன்று கருவூழ்களைக் கொண்ட, உண்மையான உடற்குழி உடைய புழுக்கள்.
  • Body elongated, worm-like and metamerically segmented into similar rings.
  • Body wall has cuticle, epidermis, circular muscles and longitudinal muscles.
  • True schizocoelic coelom is well developed and usually divided by septa.
  • Locomotion is by setae, parapodia or suckers depending on the class.
  • Alimentary canal is complete, straight and regionally differentiated.
  • Respiration occurs through moist skin, gills or parapodia.
  • Closed blood vascular system is usually present.
  • Excretion is by segmentally arranged nephridia.
  • Nervous system consists of cerebral ganglia and a double ventral nerve cord.
  • Development may include a trochophore larva in many marine forms.

2. Type Study — Nereis

Systematic position
  • Kingdom — Animalia
  • Phylum — Annelida
  • Class — Polychaeta
  • Order — Phyllodocida
  • Genus — Nereis
Habit, habitat and external morphology

Nereis is a marine polychaete worm commonly found in burrows in sand or mud along the sea shore. It is carnivorous and active, hence often called a rag worm or clam worm.

  • Body is long, narrow, cylindrical and segmented.
  • Anterior end bears a distinct head made of prostomium and peristomium.
  • Prostomium bears eyes, tentacles and palps.
  • Peristomium bears cirri and surrounds the mouth.
  • Each trunk segment bears a pair of lateral parapodia.
  • Parapodia carry setae and help in swimming, crawling and respiration.
  • Posterior end bears pygidium with anal cirri.
Parapodium of Nereis

The parapodium is biramous, with a dorsal notopodium and a ventral neuropodium. Each lobe bears bundles of chitinous setae supported by acicula. Dorsal and ventral cirri are sensory. Parapodia act as locomotory, respiratory and sensory organs.

Exam point A labelled parapodium diagram is a high-scoring addition for 5-mark and 8-mark answers.
Digestive, circulatory, excretory and nervous systems

The alimentary canal is complete. The pharynx is eversible and bears jaws for capturing prey. Circulation is closed, with dorsal and ventral blood vessels. Excretion occurs by nephridia. Nervous system consists of a brain, circumpharyngeal connectives and a double ventral nerve cord with segmental ganglia.

Reproduction and epitoky

Nereis is dioecious. During breeding, some species show epitoky, in which a non-reproductive atoke changes into a reproductive epitoke. Gametes are released into seawater and fertilization is external. Development includes a trochophore larva.

8-mark answer For Nereis type study: include systematic position, marine habitat, head region, metameric body, parapodia, digestive system with eversible pharynx, closed circulation, nephridia, nervous system, reproduction, epitoky and trochophore larva.

3. Phylum Arthropoda — General Characters

Arthropoda is the largest phylum of the animal kingdom. The name means “jointed feet”. Arthropods are triploblastic, bilaterally symmetrical, segmented coelomates with a chitinous exoskeleton, jointed appendages and haemocoel.

ஆர்த்ரோபோடா விலங்குகளின் முக்கிய அடையாளம் இணைப்புகள் கொண்ட கால்கள் மற்றும் சைட்டின் வெளிப்புற எலும்புக்கூடு ஆகும். இவை விலங்கு உலகின் மிகப்பெரிய தொகுதி.
  • Body is segmented and shows tagmatization into head, thorax and abdomen or cephalothorax and abdomen.
  • Appendages are jointed and specialized for feeding, walking, swimming, respiration or reproduction.
  • Exoskeleton is made of chitin and is periodically shed by ecdysis.
  • Coelom is reduced; main body cavity is haemocoel.
  • Alimentary canal is complete with digestive glands.
  • Respiration occurs by gills, tracheae, book lungs or body surface.
  • Circulatory system is open with dorsal heart.
  • Excretion occurs by green glands, Malpighian tubules or coxal glands.
  • Nervous system includes brain and double ventral nerve cord.
  • Sexes are usually separate; development may be direct or indirect with larval stages.

4. Type Study — Penaeus indicus (current placement: Penaeus (Fenneropenaeus) indicus)

Systematic position
  • Kingdom — Animalia
  • Phylum — Arthropoda
  • Subphylum — Crustacea
  • Class — Malacostraca
  • Order — Decapoda
  • Family — Penaeidae
  • Accepted placement — Penaeus (Fenneropenaeus) indicus
Habit, habitat and external morphology

Penaeus indicus, the familiar syllabus name for the Indian white prawn, is currently placed by WoRMS as Penaeus (Fenneropenaeus) indicus. It is a marine and estuarine crustacean of major economic importance. The body is elongated, bilaterally symmetrical and divisible into cephalothorax and abdomen.

  • Cephalothorax: covered by carapace; anteriorly produced into serrated rostrum.
  • Abdomen: six muscular segments ending in telson and uropods.
  • Eyes: compound and borne on movable stalks.
  • Antennae: antennules and antennae act as sensory organs.
  • Appendages: cephalic, thoracic and abdominal appendages are specialised.
Appendages of Penaeus

Appendages are highly specialized. Cephalic appendages include antennules, antennae, mandibles and maxillae. Thoracic appendages include maxillipeds and five pairs of walking legs. Abdominal appendages include swimmerets or pleopods, while the last pair forms uropods with the telson for backward swimming.

Respiration, circulation and excretion

Respiration occurs through gills located in the branchial chamber under the carapace. Circulation is open, with haemolymph flowing through sinuses. Excretion occurs by antennal or green glands situated near the base of the antennae.

Reproduction and development

Sexes are separate. Fertilization is external or near-external depending on species and mating condition. Development is indirect and includes larval stages such as nauplius, protozoea, zoea, mysis and post-larva. These stages are important in aquaculture and fisheries biology.

8-mark answer For Penaeus type study: include classification, habitat, cephalothorax, abdomen, carapace, rostrum, compound eyes, appendages, gills, open circulation, green glands, reproduction, larval stages and economic importance.

Peripatus and the “connecting link” syllabus concept

Peripatus belongs to the distinct phylum Onychophora. The traditional exam phrase “connecting link” refers to its mosaic of annelid-like and arthropod-like features; it is not a living intermediate or direct ancestor between the two phyla.

Insects have major beneficial and harmful roles. Pest management should therefore be selective and ecologically informed rather than based on indiscriminate insect killing.

Rice stem borer — Scirpophaga incertulas

Larvae bore into rice tillers. Vegetative-stage attack causes dead hearts; reproductive-stage attack may produce white ears/white heads with poorly filled grains. Management integrates field sanitation, destruction of infested stubbles, monitoring, conservation of natural enemies and need-based control.

Sugarcane shoot borer — Chilo infuscatellus

Larvae enter young shoots near the base and tunnel within the stem, commonly producing a dead-heart symptom. Good field hygiene, removal of affected shoots, balanced agronomy, biological control and threshold-based chemical measures form an integrated strategy.

Coconut rhinoceros beetle — Oryctes rhinoceros

Adults bore into the crown and unopened fronds of coconut palms. When injured leaves unfold, characteristic V-shaped or triangular cuts may appear. Sanitation of breeding sites, mechanical removal/trapping, biological agents and carefully targeted control are preferred components of management.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

  1. Correct pest identification and crop-stage diagnosis.
  2. Regular monitoring and economic-threshold decisions.
  3. Cultural and mechanical prevention.
  4. Conservation/augmentation of biological control.
  5. Host-plant resistance where available.
  6. Need-based, selective pesticide use with rotation of modes of action and safety precautions.
  7. Post-treatment evaluation and record keeping.
Exam distinctionIPM does not mean “no pesticides.” It means integrating compatible methods and using pesticides only when justified, at the correct dose, timing and target.

1. Phylum Mollusca — General Characters

Mollusca is one of the largest animal phyla. It includes soft-bodied, triploblastic, bilaterally symmetrical, coelomate animals such as snails, mussels, oysters, squids and octopuses. The body is usually unsegmented and is divided into head, muscular foot, visceral mass and mantle. The mantle commonly secretes a calcareous shell.

மொல்லஸ்கா விலங்குகள் மென்மையான உடலுடையவை. தலை, தசைபாதம், உள்ளுறுப்புத் தொகுதி, மான்டில் ஆகியவை முக்கிய உடல் பகுதிகள். மான்டில் பெரும்பாலும் சுண்ணாம்பு ஓடு சுரக்கும்.
  • Habitat: Mostly marine; some are freshwater and terrestrial.
  • Body: Soft, unsegmented and usually protected by a shell.
  • Mantle: A dorsal body fold that secretes the shell and encloses the mantle cavity.
  • Foot: Muscular and variously modified for creeping, burrowing or swimming.
  • Visceral mass: Contains digestive, circulatory, excretory and reproductive organs.
  • Radula: Rasping feeding organ, absent in bivalves.
  • Respiration: By ctenidia/gills in aquatic forms and pulmonary sac in terrestrial or amphibious forms.
  • Circulation: Mostly open type; cephalopods show closed circulation.
  • Excretion: By paired kidneys or metanephridia.
  • Development: Trochophore and veliger larvae occur in many marine forms.

2. Type Study — Pila globosa

Systematic position
  • Phylum — Mollusca
  • Class — Gastropoda
  • Order — Architaenioglossa (older syllabus term: Mesogastropoda)
  • Family — Ampullariidae
  • Genus — Pila
  • Species — Pila globosa
Habit and habitat

Pila globosa, the apple snail, is a freshwater gastropod found in ponds, lakes, tanks, ditches and paddy fields. It is amphibious because it can live in water as well as breathe atmospheric air. During drought or unfavourable conditions, it buries itself in mud and undergoes aestivation.

External morphology
  • The body is enclosed in a spirally coiled, globular shell.
  • The shell has whorls, spire, body whorl, aperture and columella.
  • A horny operculum closes the aperture when the animal withdraws into the shell.
  • The head bears mouth, one pair of tentacles and eyes at the base of the tentacles.
  • The broad, flat, muscular foot helps in creeping.
  • The mantle cavity contains the ctenidium and pulmonary sac.
Digestive system

The mouth leads into buccal cavity containing jaws and a radula. The radula works like a rasping organ and scrapes food. The alimentary canal includes buccal cavity, oesophagus, stomach, intestine and rectum. Digestive glands help digestion.

Respiration

Pila shows dual respiration. In water, it breathes through a ctenidium; in air, it uses the pulmonary sac. This dual respiratory adaptation explains its amphibious habit.

Circulation, excretion and reproduction

The circulatory system is open. The heart has an auricle and ventricle. Excretion occurs through the kidney. Sexes are separate, fertilization is internal, and eggs are laid in clusters above the water level.

3. Torsion, Detorsion and Pearl Formation

Torsion in Gastropods

Torsion is the 180° anti-clockwise twisting of the visceral mass, mantle cavity and shell during larval development of gastropods. Due to torsion, the mantle cavity and anus are brought from the posterior side to the anterior region above the head.

  • Occurs during veliger larval stage.
  • First phase is rapid and caused by contraction of larval retractor muscles.
  • Second phase is slower and due to differential growth.
  • It produces asymmetry and brings mantle cavity anteriorly.
  • One disadvantage is fouling, because anus and excretory openings come near the head.

Detorsion

Detorsion is the partial reversal of torsion observed in some advanced gastropods, especially opisthobranchs. It shifts the mantle cavity backwards and reduces the effects of fouling.

Pearl Formation

Pearl formation occurs in pearl oysters when a foreign particle enters between the mantle and shell. The mantle epithelium surrounds the particle and forms a pearl sac. The pearl sac secretes concentric layers of nacre around the irritant. Nacre is mainly composed of calcium carbonate and conchiolin. Over time, these layers form a pearl.

  • Foreign particle enters between mantle and shell.
  • Mantle epithelium encloses it and forms pearl sac.
  • Pearl sac deposits nacre layer by layer.
  • Natural pearls form accidentally; cultured pearls are produced by artificial implantation of nucleus.

4. Phylum Echinodermata — General Characters

Echinodermata includes exclusively marine animals such as starfish, brittle stars, sea urchins, sea cucumbers and sea lilies. Adults show pentamerous radial symmetry, while larvae are bilaterally symmetrical. The most distinctive feature is the water vascular system, which operates tube feet.

எக்கினோடெர்மாட்டா விலங்குகள் கடல்வாழ் உயிரினங்கள். பெரியவைகளில் ஐந்துபகுதி கதிர் சமச்சீர் காணப்படும்; ஆனால் இளம் நிலைகள் இருபக்கச் சமச்சீருடையவை. நீர்நாள அமைப்பு இவற்றின் தனிச்சிறப்பு.
  • All are marine and mostly free-living.
  • Adult body is pentamerous radial; larvae are bilateral.
  • Endoskeleton consists of calcareous ossicles, plates and spines.
  • Water vascular system operates tube feet.
  • No head, brain, nephridia or specialised excretory organs.
  • Digestive system is usually complete.
  • Sexes are separate; fertilization is external.
  • Regeneration power is high.

5. Type Study — Asterias

Systematic position
  • Phylum — Echinodermata
  • Class — Asteroidea
  • Order — Forcipulatida
  • Genus — Asterias
External morphology

Asterias is a starfish with a central disc and five radiating arms. The aboral surface bears spines, pedicellariae and a madreporite. The oral surface has the mouth, ambulacral grooves and rows of tube feet.

Body wall and skeleton

The body wall contains calcareous ossicles and spines. Small pincer-like pedicellariae keep the body surface clean and help in protection. Dermal branchiae or papulae help in respiration.

Digestive system

The mouth lies on the oral surface and leads to oesophagus, large stomach and intestine. The stomach has cardiac and pyloric regions. The cardiac stomach can be everted over prey during feeding.

Water vascular system

The water vascular system consists of madreporite → stone canal → ring canal → radial canals → lateral canals → ampullae and tube feet. Water enters through the madreporite and moves through this canal system. Contraction of ampullae forces fluid into tube feet, extending them; relaxation retracts them. It helps in locomotion, attachment, food capture, respiration and sensory function.

Exam point Write the parts in correct order: madreporite → stone canal → ring canal → radial canal → lateral canal → ampulla → tube foot.
Reproduction and development

Sexes are separate. Fertilization is external. Development includes free-swimming bilaterally symmetrical larvae such as bipinnaria and brachiolaria, which later metamorphose into radially symmetrical adults.

Syllabus Completion — Economic Importance of Cephalopods

Cephalopods such as squids, cuttlefishes and octopuses are important marine fishery resources and high-value foods. They support capture fisheries, processing and export industries; cuttlefish bone has commercial uses; cephalopod nervous systems and behaviour are important in neurobiology, sensory biology and comparative cognition. Ecologically they are active predators and also important prey for fishes, marine mammals and seabirds, linking trophic levels in marine food webs. Sustainable harvest requires species identification, size/season regulation where applicable, by-catch reduction and stock monitoring.

Cuttlefish bone வணிகப் பயன்பாடு உடையது. தலைக்காலிகளின் நரம்பு மண்டலம், உணர்வு மற்றும் சிக்கலான நடத்தை neurobiology, sensory biology, comparative cognition ஆராய்ச்சிகளில் முக்கிய மாதிரியாகப் பயன்படுகின்றன. கடல் உணவுச் சங்கிலியில் இவை வேட்டையாடிகளாகவும், மீன்கள், கடல் பாலூட்டிகள் மற்றும் கடற்பறவைகளுக்கான இரையாகவும் trophic இணைப்பை உருவாக்குகின்றன. நீடித்த பயன்பாட்டிற்கு சரியான இன அடையாளம், stock monitoring, தேவையான அளவு/காலக் கட்டுப்பாடுகள் மற்றும் by-catch குறைப்பு அவசியம்.

§Practical Examination Corner

Spotter identification, diagnostic characters, important labels and viva focus for major type studies.

1-Mark MCQs: identify structures, classes, examples and key functions.

Paramecium

  • Spotter: freshwater ciliate protozoan.
  • Diagnostic characters: slipper-shaped body, cilia, oral groove, macronucleus, micronucleus, contractile vacuoles.
  • Labels: cilia, oral groove, cytostome, macronucleus, micronucleus, food vacuole, contractile vacuole.
  • Viva focus: heterokaryosis, osmoregulation, conjugation, ciliary locomotion.

Sycon

  • Spotter: marine calcareous sponge.
  • Diagnostic characters: sessile body, ostia, osculum, spicules and syconoid canal system.
  • Labels: ostium, incurrent canal, prosopyle, radial canal, apopyle, spongocoel, osculum.
  • Viva focus: canal system, choanocytes, spicules and sponge reproduction.

Obelia

  • Spotter: colonial hydrozoan cnidarian.
  • Diagnostic characters: branching colony, hydranth, gonangium, perisarc and coenosarc.
  • Labels: hydranth, tentacles, hypostome, gonangium, medusa buds, perisarc, coenosarc.
  • Viva focus: polymorphism, metagenesis, planula larva and alternation of generations.

Fasciola hepatica

  • Spotter: liver fluke, digenetic trematode.
  • Diagnostic characters: dorsoventrally flattened leaf-like body, oral sucker, ventral sucker and branched reproductive organs.
  • Labels: oral sucker, ventral sucker, pharynx, intestine, uterus, ovary, testes, vitellaria.
  • Viva focus: digenetic life cycle, snail intermediate host, cercaria, metacercaria and fascioliasis.

Ascaris lumbricoides

  • Spotter: intestinal roundworm.
  • Diagnostic characters: cylindrical unsegmented body, sexual dimorphism, pseudocoelom and thick cuticle.
  • Labels: mouth, lips, intestine, ovary/testis, uterus, cloaca, spicules, curved male tail.
  • Viva focus: monogenetic life cycle, infective egg, pulmonary migration and ascariasis.

Nereis

  • Spotter: marine polychaete annelid.
  • Diagnostic characters: metameric segmentation, parapodia, setae, prostomium and eversible pharynx.
  • Labels: prostomium, peristomium, parapodia, setae, tentacles, cirri.
  • Viva focus: metamerism, parapodium, trochophore larva and epitoky.

Penaeus indicus

  • Spotter: marine prawn, crustacean arthropod.
  • Diagnostic characters: cephalothorax, abdomen, carapace, rostrum, jointed appendages and gills.
  • Labels: rostrum, carapace, antennae, walking legs, swimmerets, telson, uropods.
  • Viva focus: appendage modification, green glands, gills and larval stages.

Pila globosa

  • Spotter: freshwater apple snail.
  • Diagnostic characters: coiled shell, operculum, muscular foot, mantle cavity and torsion.
  • Labels: shell, apex, spire, aperture, operculum, foot, tentacle, eye.
  • Viva focus: torsion, shell morphology, radula, respiration and pearl formation concept.

Asterias

  • Spotter: starfish, echinoderm.
  • Diagnostic characters: pentaradial adult symmetry, tube feet, madreporite, ossicles and water vascular system.
  • Labels: central disc, arms, madreporite, ambulacral groove, tube feet, spines.
  • Viva focus: water vascular system, tube foot mechanism, regeneration and bipinnaria larva.

§About this App

Design, content and acknowledgements

The Invertebrata Study Companion is a fully offline, single-file HTML application designed for undergraduate B.Sc. Zoology students. It follows the standard Indian university syllabus for the paper Invertebrata (Non-Chordata).

Learning App v3.0The course now uses guided retrieval checkpoints, adaptive difficult-concept practice, scheduled review, concept mastery and a projector-ready Teacher Mode. Progress is based on demonstrated responses rather than page opening alone.

Features:

  • Unit-wise structured content with strict academic rigor.
  • Complete English lessons with an aligned Tamil concept companion; v2.8 syllabus-completion and interactive laboratory modules are fully parallel in English and Tamil, while legacy lesson sections retain their established companion-depth Tamil treatment.
  • Type-study format for each major organism — 14 structured sections.
  • Labelled inline SVG diagrams requiring no external images.
  • Self-assessment MCQs with explanations and a score tracker.
  • Searchable glossary and question bank.
  • Bookmark and progress tracking via localStorage (optional; disabled if unavailable).
  • Mobile-first, responsive, print-friendly design.

Technology: Pure HTML + inline CSS + inline JavaScript. The downloaded lesson app uses no external libraries or CDNs and runs offline; supplementary resource links require internet access. No service-worker background caching is claimed for file:// use.

Offline single-file use (v2.8.1)The downloaded HTML contains the complete application, labelled animal diagrams, controlled movement animations, interactive life-cycle/spotter laboratories and local mastery analytics, and can be opened directly without internet access. Supplementary web resources still require a connection. Automatic installation prompts and service-worker background caching are not claimed for the locally opened file.
Spaced review (v2.5)Every MCQ Practice question you answer — in a unit page or the pooled MCQ Practice view — is scheduled for review automatically using a simplified SM-2 algorithm (correct answers push the next review further out; wrong answers bring it back tomorrow). The "Spaced Review" card on the Home page shows what's due and starts a review session pulling only those questions. Nothing to configure; it runs entirely from answers you've already given.
Tamil displaySince v2.3 the application embeds its own Tamil font (Noto Sans Tamil, Tamil-block subset) directly in the file, so Tamil script renders identically on every device — including phones without a Tamil font installed — with no internet access required.

Content basis: Mind-map notes of the Dept. of Zoology, Govt. Arts & Science College, Nagercoil; supplemented by standard reference texts (Kotpal, Jordan & Verma, Ekambaranatha Ayyar, Barnes).
